Pillar 02 · Language · Release 03

If your team doesn't share a language, you don't have a pipeline. You have a rumor.

Two DJs on the same stage, mixing in different keys at different tempos, is not a set — it is a train wreck. Two reps in the same pipeline review, using "qualified" to mean two different things, is the commercial equivalent. The crowd feels the first within four bars. Leadership feels the second at quarter end, when the forecast collapses.

The first pillar, Vision, answers where we play and how we win. Language answers something more mundane and more brutal: do we mean the same thing when we say the same word? Growth is not accidental. It is an architecture — and every architecture starts with a shared vocabulary.

The cost of speaking in dialects

Without a defined commercial language, every rep builds a private one. "Discovery is done" means a demo happened. Or a champion said "interesting." Or nothing at all. Multiply that by twelve reps and three regions, and your CRM is no longer a system of record. It is a collection of opinions.

The research on this is older than most SaaS playbooks, and it has held up:

18%

revenue-growth gap between companies that clearly define their sales process and those that don't — 28% for those mastering three pipeline practices. Jordan & Kelly, HBR 2015

+Win rate

organizations moving from informal to formally defined, adopted processes consistently outperform on win rates — by chasing fewer deals that were never real. CSO Insights

60–70%

of B2B sales content goes unused — not because it's bad, but because nobody agreed which word, stage, or buyer situation it maps to. Forrester

And there is a human cost that rarely makes the dashboard: when milestone definitions shift with whoever is asking, reps live in permanent ambiguity. Ambiguity is exhausting. People don't burn out from high standards. They burn out from moving ones.

Language across the three dimensions

In the OTGE grid, every pillar is scored across People, Process and Data. Language is where the three either lock together — or quietly drift apart.

People. A shared language is a coaching tool before it is a reporting tool. If "champion" has a definition — someone with personal stakes who sells internally when you're not in the room — a manager can coach to it: "Who is your champion, and what have they done without you this month?" Without the definition, the same conversation is vibes.

Process. Stage names are not language. Exit criteria are. And the critical design rule: exit criteria must be customer-verifiable, not seller-declared. "I sent the proposal" is seller activity. "The customer confirmed the evaluation committee and decision date in writing" is evidence. Deals advance on evidence, never on optimism.

Data. Your forecast is only as honest as your taxonomy. When "Commit" means the same thing in Antwerp, Amsterdam and Aachen, forecasts roll up clean. When it doesn't, you're not forecasting — you're averaging emotions. This is also the unlock for any serious use of AI on revenue data: models trained on undefined fields automate the confusion, faster.

The instrument: the Commercial Dictionary

Every pillar in this series ships one working instrument. For Language it is the simplest and the most resisted: a one-page Commercial Dictionary, owned by revenue leadership, versioned like a product.

Four sections. One page.

  1. Core terms (max 15). Lead, MQL, SQL, opportunity, champion, economic buyer, closed-lost categories. One sentence each. If a definition needs a paragraph, it isn't a definition yet.
  2. Stage exit criteria. Per stage, 2–3 verifiable conditions phrased as customer evidence. No exceptions for the top rep, no exceptions at quarter end — especially not at quarter end.
  3. Forecast categories. What Commit, Best Case and Pipeline mean, with the evidence threshold for each.
  4. The retirement list. Words banned for meaninglessness. Nominees: "engaged," "warm," and "they loved the demo."

Rollout matters more than authorship. Draft it with your senior reps, not for them — people defend what they help build. Then make it operational in three places: the CRM (fields mirror the dictionary), the weekly pipeline review (deals challenged against exit criteria, in the dictionary's words), and onboarding (day one, before the product deck).

This sits deliberately on top of the revenue architecture of Jacco van der Kooij / Winning by Design: the bow tie gives you the physics of recurring revenue; the Commercial Dictionary makes sure your whole company describes those physics in one tongue. Two DJs, one key, one tempo. That's when a room starts to move.
